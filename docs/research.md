# Build the Porch for comfort, not cure

The evidence supports building The Front Porch, but only as an honest promise: **a few gentle, familiar, impossible-to-fail things to enjoy, ideally with someone you love beside you.** It does not support any claim that Garden, Match the Socks or Sing-Along improves memory, slows decline or calms agitation, and the best-evidenced non-drug programmes (group cognitive stimulation, reminiscence, music therapy) are facilitated by trained people, give small effects that fade, and have never been tested as a solo phone app. Of the three activities, **Sing-Along has the most backing**: the 2025 Cochrane review finds music activities probably lift depressive symptoms a little, and the strongest single trial had a carer and the person singing together at home. **Match the Socks** rests on Montessori-style matching and the idea that people need meaningful occupation, but nobody has ever tested an on-screen chore. **Garden** has the weakest outcome evidence: the one meta-analysis that split hands-on gardening from looking at plants found that looking did *not* reduce agitation, so Garden earns its place as the activity that works for anyone, even late in the illness, and as a pleasant thing to share, not as a calming treatment. The shared design rules are better supported than the outcomes: tap targets of at least 14 mm and ideally 20 mm or more, tap-only input, no drag, no timers, no scores, no wrong-answer sounds, adult language, large high-contrast text, one level of navigation and no data collected. Every number below is tagged with how strong its evidence is, and where a number is design judgement rather than research, the report says so.

**How to read the grades.** The four research teams used slightly different scales; this report folds them into one. **Strong** means a Cochrane or large systematic review with moderate-to-high certainty, a national guideline, or a formal standard. **Moderate** means several trials or a review pointing the same way with low-to-moderate certainty. **Weak** means small trials, pre/post studies, one lab study, or scoping reviews. **Expert** means charity guidance, practitioner advice, ethics reports or the researchers' own design inference, with no direct test.

## Enjoyment and company are the only claims the evidence will carry

The strongest non-drug programme for dementia is **cognitive stimulation therapy (CST)**: a Cochrane review of 37 trials and 2,766 people found it "probably results in a small benefit to cognition at the end of the course of sessions" in mild-to-moderate dementia ([Cochrane CD005562.pub3](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD005562.pub3/information)). That is the ceiling, and it does not transfer: CST is group, therapist-led discussion, and **only one of the 37 trials used an app** ([Alzheimer Europe](https://www.alzheimer-europe.org/news/cochrane-review-analyses-studies-cognitive-stimulation-finding-small-benefits-people-mild)). Reminiscence therapy, the nearest relative of "a song from your youth", showed **no effect on agitation** (SMD 0.03) and no effect on depression across all settings, with small gains only in care homes and one-to-one formats ([Cochrane CD001120.pub3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6494367)). Music therapy is the best-studied activity relevant to the Porch: the 2025 Cochrane update (30 trials, 1,720 people) finds a **small improvement in depressive symptoms, SMD −0.23, moderate certainty**, but agitation SMD −0.05, which is moderate-certainty evidence of *no* effect, no clear benefit for anxiety, wellbeing or cognition, and no evidence that anything lasts after sessions stop ([Cochrane CD003477](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003477_does-music-based-therapy-help-people-dementia)). Even the well-known "tailored activities" approach, the Tailored Activity Program, found **no benefit on agitation in its largest trial of 250 families**, though carers needed to do less and felt better ([BMC Geriatrics 2021](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s12877-021-02511-4.pdf)).

Tablet studies specifically are feasibility-grade. In an open-label study of 36 inpatients, "all participants, regardless of dementia severity, were able to use apps", but **the more severe the dementia, the simpler the app had to be** ([ScienceDaily on Vahia et al. 2017](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/01/170107113239.htm)). A scoping review of 17 studies links tablets to more engagement and enjoyment ([Hung et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8044627)), and a systematic review of touchscreen interventions reports tentative benefits for carers' sense of burden and for the relationship ([Tyack & Camic 2017](https://repository.canterbury.ac.uk/item/88273/touchscreen-interventions-and-the-wellbeing-of-people-with-dementia-and-caregivers-a-systematic-review)). None of these were controlled trials of a home app, and in the agitation studies, agitation fell with *any* engaging stimulus, including a nostalgic movie ([Reynolds et al. 2018](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/article-pdfs/Reynolds-et-al-2018-KPS-CHD.pdf)). The pattern across all of this is that **something engaging, social and enjoyable helps people in the moment**, and music is one good vehicle for it. That is a real benefit worth building for. It is just not a treatment.

The claims to avoid are therefore specific. The UK's national guideline says **"Do not offer cognitive training to treat mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease"** ([NICE NG97 1.4.7](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng97/chapter/Recommendations)). Cochrane found **no trial of computerised brain training in mild cognitive impairment that even measured whether dementia developed** ([Cochrane CD012279](https://www.cochrane.org/CD012279/DEMENTIA_computerised-cognitive-training-preventing-dementia-people-mild-cognitive-impairment)). The US Federal Trade Commission made Lumosity pay **$2 million** for implying its games protected against dementia, and named its purchase of "dementia" and "Alzheimer's" ad keywords ([FTC 2016](https://ftc.gov/news-events/press-releases/2016/01/lumosity-pay-2-million-settle-ftc-deceptive-advertising-charges)). The much-publicised 2026 ACTIVE result (40% vs 49% dementia diagnoses after speed training with boosters) applied to people who were healthy at the start, used supervised training, and lists the software maker as a conflict of interest ([NIH](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/cognitive-speed-training-over-weeks-may-delay-diagnosis-dementia-over-decades)); it says nothing about people who already have dementia. And Sea Hero Quest, a research game played by over 4.3 million people, was widely misread as an Alzheimer's test and as protection against the disease ([The Psychologist](https://thepsychologist.bps.org.uk/new-gaming-app-generates-unprecedented-levels-data-dementia-research)). So the Porch should never say "brain training", "exercise your brain", "keeps your mind sharp", "slows decline", "improves memory", "therapy", "treatment" or "reduces agitation", should never show a score or a "brain age", and should never advertise on disease keywords with a benefit pitch. What it can say truthfully is: **gentle, pleasant things to do together; no wrong answers; familiar tasks; songs people know.**

## Shared shell rules: 20 mm targets, one tap, nothing to fail

These rules apply to the porch and to every activity. The motor and vision numbers are the best-supported numbers in the whole report; the session and sound rules rest mostly on expert guidance.

A practical conversion note (the researchers' own arithmetic, not a source): a CSS pixel is not a fixed size. On a standard iPad, 1 mm is about 5.2 CSS px; on an iPhone, about 6.4. So **14 mm ≈ 73 px on iPad, 90 px on iPhone; 20 mm ≈ 105 / 125 px; 30 mm ≈ 157 / 185 px.** Size targets in millimetres and convert per device.

For people with motor impairments, **errors kept falling from 11% at 20 mm to 7.5% at 30 mm** ([Chen et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3572909)). | Navigation | **One level deep**: porch → activity → a "Back to the porch" button always visible in the same place. No menus, scrolling, carousels, pop-ups or hidden gestures in the person's path. | Guidance converges on shallow, linear navigation with a visible Home and Back; "a back button is reported as the most used button" ([Vo 2023](https://essay.utwente.nl/95898/1/Vo_BA_EEMCS.pdf); [W3C COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/design_guide.html)). Auto-moving and flashing content is "likely to cause task abandonment" ([Smart Interface Design Patterns](https://smart-interface-design-patterns.com/articles/designing-for-dementia)). No study gives a numeric limit on choices per screen; three is well within every guideline. | **Expert** (consistent across sources) |
| Labels | **Every tappable thing is a realistic picture with a word next to it.** | "Add text labels to icons" and use concrete, familiar images ([JMIR mHealth 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10557006)); people with dementia "may be unable to choose the correct... icon" ([JMIR 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10603562)). | **Weak** |
| Text size and font | **Body text at least 22 pt on a tablet; porch labels and lyrics about 30 pt or more**; bold or semi-bold sans-serif; sentence case, no all-caps. | On a 9.7-inch tablet, adults 65+ did best with **22 pt** buttons vs 10 pt ([font-size review 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9376262)); one guideline asks for **30 pt for critical text, 20 pt for secondary** ([JMIR mHealth 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10557006)); dementia experts asked for bold, sans-serif type ([Vo 2023](https://essay.utwente.nl/95898/1/Vo_BA_EEMCS.pdf); [AbilityNet](https://abilitynet.org.uk/factsheets/designing-dementia)). No dementia-specific font study exists. | **Weak** |
| Contrast and colour | **Text contrast at least 4.5:1, aim for 7:1.** Strong *lightness* contrast between anything tappable and its background; check every screen in greyscale. Never make blue vs green, or blue vs grey, the only difference between two things. Avoid blue for selected states. | WCAG sets 4.5:1 for text ([AbilityNet](https://abilitynet.org.uk/factsheets/designing-dementia)); 7:1 is the researchers' choice, not tested in dementia. The ageing lens yellows and blues and greens become hard to tell apart ([MDPI 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/16/23/10251)); "tone contrast matters more than hue" ([Dementia Alliance International](https://dementiaallianceinternational.org/blog/colour-and-pattern-in-dementia-care-environments)). | **Weak** (phenomenon) / **Expert** (design advice) |
| Backgrounds and images | Plain, mid-light backgrounds; **no dark patches, strong patterns, shadows, reflections or glossy effects**; keep important content near the centre; slow motion only. | People with dementia can see a dark doormat as "a deep black hole" and shadows as animals or people, even with healthy eyes ([Alzheimer's Society](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/about-dementia/stages-and-symptoms/how-dementia-changes-perception); [AFA](https://alzfdn.org/dementia-changes-visual-impairment)); tunnel vision and trouble tracking fast movement are common ([NY Memory Center](https://www.nymemorycenter.org/post/dementia-and-visual-perception)). These come from rooms, not screens. | **Expert** |
| Spoken prompts | Short, plain, active sentences ("Tap a flower."); natural pace; a lower-pitched voice; the same words on screen; a replay button. | People with Alzheimer's understood better when speech was **repeated, but not when it was slowed** ([Small, Kemper & Lyons 1997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9100263)); age-related hearing loss makes higher-pitched voices harder ([Medscape](https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/855989-overview)). | **Weak** |
| Sound | **Sound is never the only channel. No music under speech.** Volume starts moderate, with a big obvious control; no sudden loud sounds; nothing autoplays loud or fast. | Background music worsens speech understanding for people with hearing loss ([Iowa protocols](https://iowaprotocols.medicine.uiowa.edu/protocols/music-therapy-older-adults-hearing-loss-0)); loud noise can overstimulate or frighten ([Encompass Health](https://www.encompasshealth.com/health-resources/articles/decreasing-distress-in-dementia-patients)). | **Expert** |
| Feedback | Immediate, gentle, visual plus a soft sound; **dignified, never patronising**: a flower opening, "That's a pair.", "Lovely." No "Good job!", "Well done, sweetie!", stars, confetti, cartoon mascots or toddler colours. | In nursing homes, resistance to care was **0.55 with elderspeak vs 0.26 with normal talk** ([Williams et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2823803)); a hospital study found elderspeak in **96.6%** of encounters and a modifiable cause of refused care ([JAGS 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35642656/)). The Alzheimer's Society calls childlike talk patronising and infantilising ([Alzheimer's Society](https://alzheimers.org.uk/blog/language-dementia-what-not-to-say)). Transfer from human carers to app text is an inference. | **Weak** (human studies) / **Expert** (for apps) |
| No failure | **No scores, levels, streaks, "wrong" sounds, red crosses, shakes, "Try again", or test-like questions** ("What year is this song from?", "Name this flower"). | Errorless methods beat trial-and-error in dementia, **effect size 0.65** ([Roberts, Jones & Clare meta-analysis](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/portal/files/20577677/null)); a review found errorless learning works for everyday tasks ([de Werd et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3775624)), though a later review was more mixed ([Ferry 2021](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/82488)). | Session length | **Open-ended; natural stopping points every two or three minutes** (a song ends, the garden fills, the basket is done); leaving is never penalised; the app reopens where it left off. | **No study gives an ideal session length or attention span in minutes.** Self-paced breaks reduced fatigue and stress for people with tremor ([Montague et al.](http://web.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/hugo.nicolau/publications/2014/Montague-ASSETS-2014.pdf)); dementia fluctuates day to day ([Smart Interface Design Patterns](https://smart-interface-design-patterns.com/articles/designing-for-dementia)); effects of music fade when sessions stop, which argues for easy, frequent short returns ([Cochrane CD003477](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003477_does-music-based-therapy-help-people-dementia)). | **Expert** (a genuine evidence gap) |
| Calm by default; evening | No flashing; animations slow (a bloom over about 1–2 s); no notifications, badges or reminders. Optional softer, warmer look after a local evening hour, with Garden and gentle songs first. Never sold as a sleep aid. | The Alzheimer's Association advises reducing evening stimulation and offering calm music or photos ([Alz.org tip sheet](https://alz.org/getmedia/d929574d-96ab-4d27-9da9-1224cbc19233/alzheimers-dementia-sundowning-ts.pdf)). Light research conflicts (bright light *raised* agitation in one 66-person study, [PMC3711022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3711022)), and a tablet's light is far below therapy doses, so the evening concern is stimulation, not light. The 1–2 s bloom is a design choice. | **Expert** |
| Nothing in the way | **No login, no account, works offline once loaded, no install banners, permission prompts or update notices in the person's path; no links that leave the app.** Works on a table or stand. | Barriers that end sessions are logins, power, connectivity, unfamiliar devices and difficulty holding the tablet ([Hung et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8044627); [PMC7691942](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7691942); [UAIS 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10209-020-00745-4)). People with dementia accidentally edit home screens with long presses ([JMIR 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10603562)). | **Weak** |
| Tablet over phone | Recommend a tablet where the family has one; on a phone, show **fewer items, not smaller ones**. | Older adults made **3.75% errors on a tablet vs 7.8% on a phone** in a tremor-sensitive task ([Nicolau & Jorge 2012](http://web.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/hugo.nicolau/publications/2012/Nicolau-ASSETS-2012.pdf)). | **Weak** |
| Privacy | **No data collected, no analytics, no third-party trackers** (check fonts, CDNs and any audio hosting). | The Nuffield Council flags monitoring as an intrusion to weigh case by case ([Nuffield 2009](https://www.nuffieldbioethics.org/assets/pdfs/Nuffield-Dementia-short-guide.pdf)); privacy and stigma are named concerns about dementia apps ([JMIR 2023](https://www.jmir.org/2023/1/e46188)). | **Expert** (authoritative ethics) |

One standard was not checked by the research team: WCAG's rule against content that flashes more than three times a second (2.3.1) is a general seizure-safety standard, cited here from background knowledge. The Porch should follow it anyway, since it costs nothing.

## Each activity, piece by piece

### Garden: keep it, as the one thing anybody can do

Garden's honest job is narrower than it first looks. The best gardening evidence is for **hands-on, participatory horticulture**, which improved agitation, positive emotion and engagement in nursing homes; **ornamental horticulture (looking at plants) did not improve agitation or positive emotion** ([Zhao et al. meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10623907)). Tapping a flower on a screen is much closer to looking than to digging. Virtual nature has only tiny studies, and in the best of them (14 residents) **agitation fell just as much watching a nostalgic movie** as with nature ([Reynolds et al. 2018](https://www.healthdesign.org/sites/default/files/article-pdfs/Reynolds-et-al-2018-KPS-CHD.pdf)). The closest relative to "tap and something lovely happens" is Tovertafel, a table-top light projection game: in 25 residents with moderate to severe dementia it partly reduced apathy and increased social interaction, but the study had no control group and the authors warn the results "might be overestimated" ([Konrad et al. 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11638534/)). Tovertafel is also a shared, table-sized experience, and a phone is small and solitary.

So why keep Garden? Because it is the only activity on the Porch that **still works when the others stop working**. Simpler apps suit more severe dementia ([Vahia et al.](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/01/170107113239.htm)); Montessori-style activities raised engagement even in advanced dementia ([Orsulic-Jeras et al. 2000](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2685273)); the NHS stresses that it is "often assumed that people in the later stages of dementia are unable to engage in activities, but this isn't true" ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/content-widgets/health-a-to-z/dementia/living-with-dementia/activities)). A garden where every touch succeeds is errorless by construction. And it gives a son or daughter something to talk about ("Did you grow roses at the old house?"), which is where the shared-use evidence below says the real benefit lives. Garden's stated purpose should be **enjoyment, a calm moment and a conversation opener**, and nothing more.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Talk prompts for the carer | Optional, kept in the carer notes rather than on the person's screen | Shared use is where the evidence for benefit is | Touchscreen co-use, **Weak** ([Tyack & Camic](https://repository.canterbury.ac.uk/item/88273/touchscreen-interventions-and-the-wellbeing-of-people-with-dementia-and-caregivers-a-systematic-review); [CIRCA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6153376)) | **Cut** on-screen questions such as "What flower is this?" (a recall test) |
| What Garden claims | "Something pretty to do; every touch makes a flower." | Honesty | Ornamental nature did not reduce agitation, **Moderate** ([Zhao](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10623907)) | **Cut** "calming", "relaxing therapy", "reduces agitation" |

One caveat on the citations: the research team could not confirm which of two PubMed Central IDs belongs to the Lu and the Zhao meta-analyses ([PMC9517764](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9517764) and [PMC10623907](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10623907)). Check before quoting either publicly.

### Match the Socks: keep it, as a familiar job done with dignity

Matching pairs of socks is a standard Montessori activity for dementia, alongside sorting buttons by colour ([NCCDP](https://www.nccdp.org/activities-for-people-living-with-dementia/); [Dementia Australia](https://www.dementia.org.au/about-dementia/dementia-expert-webinars/webinar-montessori-approach-activities-and-dementia)). Montessori-based programming raised engagement in a 15-study review of care homes ([Yan et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10336713)), though evidence for any effect on thinking is "weak" ([Sheppard et al. 2016](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/06c96e38-aa6a-45ad-88f4-262ec3d8daba)). The deeper reason is Cohen-Mansfield's finding that **the most common unmet needs behind agitation are meaningful activity, company and relief from pain**, and that activities matched to a person's past identity and abilities reduced agitation in nursing homes ([TREA, PMC4451402](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4451402)). Folding laundry fits many people's past identity.

Two findings keep this honest. First, **nobody has studied a digital chore**: whether tapping pictures of socks carries any of the benefit of handling real socks is unknown. Second, familiarity alone does not make a game usable. When 30 people with dementia were handed a touchscreen, **only 17% got through familiar Solitaire, against 93% for a simple new game**, and they enjoyed both regardless of success ([Astell et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26897552)). Familiar framing is a reason to choose socks; it is not a substitute for making the mechanics trivially easy. There is also a dignity risk borrowed from the doll-therapy debate, where families and staff felt dolls "infantilised the adult and compromised dignity" ([NPR 2016](https://www.npr.org/sections/health-shots/2016/10/03/495655678/doll-therapy-may-help-calm-people-with-dementia-but-it-has-critics)) and caused **distress to family members** ([QUB](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/ethical-considerations-of-doll-therapy-for-people-with-dementia/)). The risk is in the presentation, not the task. Match the Socks should be framed as what it is, a game, not as "helping with the laundry", which would imply real work is being done. That keeps it at the honest end of the truth-telling guidance, which says carers should aim to tell the truth unless the truth would cause needless distress ([Mental Health Foundation 2016](https://www.mentalhealth.org.uk/explore-mental-health/publications/what-truth-inquiry-about-truth-and-lying-dementia-care)).

| Element | What it does | Why it exists | Evidence and grade | Cut or changed because of the evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Socks always face-up | Every sock is visible all the time | Matching by sight, no memory load | Montessori matching, **Weak** ([PMC2685273](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2685273)); "ensure processes do not rely on memory", **Expert** ([W3C COGA](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/design_guide.html)) | **Cut** the face-down "concentration" memory game entirely; it is a recall test |
| Tap one sock, then its partner | Two taps pair them; the pair folds into the basket | Works with tremor and slowness | Taps preferred over drag; drag frustrating in Parkinson's, **Moderate** ([Nunes](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/6621/1/PAS_user%20interface%202015.pdf)) | **Cut** drag-and-drop |

| Pairs differ by pattern and lightness | Stripes vs spots vs plain, light vs dark | Pairs must be obvious at a glance | Blue/green confusion in ageing eyes, **Weak** ([MDPI 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/16/23/10251)); tone over hue, **Expert** ([DAI](https://dementiaallianceinternational.org/blog/colour-and-pattern-in-dementia-care-environments)) | **Cut** pairs that differ only by colour, especially navy vs black or blue vs green |

The 2–4 figure is a design inference. | **Cut** levels, unlocks and difficulty that rises automatically |
| A mismatch does nothing | A non-matching second tap just un-highlights; the first sock gently settles back | No failure state | Errorless learning, **Moderate** ([Roberts et al.](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/portal/files/20577677/null)) | **Cut** buzzer, red cross, shake, "Try again" |
| Adult socks | Real-looking adult socks: argyle, work socks, nylon knee-highs | Fits a grown-up's past; avoids looking like a toddler toy | Patronising reactions to simple activities are real and split, **Expert** ([Alzheimer's Society](https://www.alzheimers.org.uk/dementia-professionals/dementia-experience-toolkit/research-methods/tips-dementia-friendly-group)) | **Cut** cartoon socks, faces, rainbow toddler patterns |
| "All matched" moment | Basket full, plain words: "All paired up." | Recognise a job done, a natural stop | Feedback without elderspeak, **Weak** ([Williams 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2823803)) | No stars, no "Great job!", no timer or speed record |
| Turn-taking | Two people can take turns on one screen | Co-use is where the benefit is | **Weak** ([Tyack & Camic](https://repository.canterbury.ac.uk/item/88273/touchscreen-interventions-and-the-wellbeing-of-people-with-dementia-and-caregivers-a-systematic-review)) | Nothing marks whose turn it is or keeps count |
| Name and framing | "Match the Socks" | Honest game framing | Truth-telling default, **Expert** ([MHF](https://www.mentalhealth.org.uk/explore-mental-health/publications/what-truth-inquiry-about-truth-and-lying-dementia-care)) | **Cut** "Help with the laundry" or anything that implies real work |

Match the Socks should be **hidden or de-emphasised for late-stage users**: every source mapping activity to stage puts matching beyond severe dementia (the researchers' inference, since no source maps app types to stages).

### Sing-Along: keep it, and make it a duet first

Sing-Along stands on the firmest ground. Brain regions that hold long-known music show **relatively little atrophy and metabolic loss in Alzheimer's** ([Jacobsen et al. 2015, *Brain*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26041611/)), which helps explain why old songs survive, though how much is spared varies between people ([*Brain* commentary](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/138/8/2122/330617)). The strongest relevant trial randomised **89 pairs of a person with dementia and their carer** to 10 weeks of coached singing, coached listening or usual care; both music groups improved mood and orientation, and **singing also improved short-term and working memory and the carer's wellbeing** ([Särkämö et al. 2014](https://culturecase.kdl.kcl.ac.uk/research/2016/06/music-activities-benefit-dementia-patients-and-their-carers)). Singing helped most in mild dementia, listening more in moderate dementia ([Särkämö 2016](https://content.iospress.com/articles/journal-of-alzheimers-disease/jad150453)). The limits are just as clear: Cochrane finds **no effect on agitation** and no lasting effects ([CD003477](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003477_does-music-based-therapy-help-people-dementia)); personalised playlists in a 54-home, 976-resident trial **did not change agitation or drug use** ([METRIcAL](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9256757)); and for Parkinson's, a 2023 review found "no evidence of efficacy" for music on thinking ([PMC10415237](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10415237)).

For Parkinson's, the transferable benefit is the voice. In-person group singing (ParkinSong) made weekly singers **5.13 dB louder** than controls over 12 months and improved voice-related quality of life ([Tamplin et al. 2020](https://content.iospress.com/articles/journal-of-parkinsons-disease/jpd191838)), but a small **online** version found no improvement ([RCSLT draft summary](https://www.rcslt.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/DRAFT-Interventions-for-speech-difficulties_research.pdf)), so "sing out" is an invitation, not a therapy. People with Parkinson's often struggle to tap in time with a beat ([Grahn & Brett 2009](https://www.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/publications/articles/6716/)), and people with Parkinson's dementia did not speed their walking with faster beats ([PMC6744333](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6744333)), so tapping must never be scored and Sing-Along must never become a walking exercise.

## Song rules: pre-1931, family-sung, recorded by us

The era problem is real. Music memory peaks for songs heard at **roughly ages 10–30, peaking around 14–15** ([Kaiser 2022](https://pure.au.dk/ws/files/338096928/WIRES_Cognitive_Science_-_2022_-_Kaiser.pdf)), so for parents born about 1930–1956 the most powerful songs are popular hits from about 1940–1985, nearly all still under copyright. Two findings open a legitimate door to older, free material. The **"cascading reminiscence bump"** shows people also remember their *parents'* music, heard during childhood ([Krumhansl & Zupnick 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797613486486/)), replicated in a 470-person study for music up to about two decades before birth ([Jakubowski et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10357890)). And songs learned at school, church, ball games and holidays were sung for decades afterwards (an inference, not a tested finding). The cost is honest: only **personally significant** songs formed a reminiscence bump in one study ([Rathbone et al. 2017](https://radar.brookes.ac.uk/radar/file/0f812b07-869a-404f-82f7-388684fd83b2/1/rathbone2017tracks.pdf)), and older adults' song cues often bring back general rather than specific memories ([Kaiser 2022](https://pure.au.dk/ws/files/338096928/WIRES_Cognitive_Science_-_2022_-_Kaiser.pdf)), so expect these songs to land a little softer than a parent's own teenage hits. That is one more reason finish-the-line must stay gentle.

The copyright rule in the US is simple: **as of 1 January 2026, music published in 1930 or earlier is public domain, while sound recordings are free only up to 1925** ([Duke CSPD](https://web.law.duke.edu/cspd/publicdomainday/2026/); [Library of Congress](https://blogs.loc.gov/copyright/2025/12/lifecycle-of-copyright-1930-works-in-the-public-domain/)). That means the Porch must **use its own recordings**, and must use the melody and words as first published, because later verses and arrangements can carry their own copyright. "Happy Birthday" is safe: a 2016 settlement left it in the public domain ([Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/happy-birthday-fight-settles-for-14-million/)). "Bye Bye Blackbird" (1926) became public domain in 2022, though recordings of it remain protected ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bye_Bye_Blackbird)). **"You Are My Sunshine" is not public domain**: it was registered in 1940 and renewed, so treat it as protected until about 2035 ([sub-q.com](https://sub-q.com/who-owns-you-are-my-sunshine/)). The research team also lists "God Bless America" (1939), "Over the Rainbow" (1939), "White Christmas" (1942), "Let It Snow" (1945), "Rudolph" (1949) and "Frosty" (1950) as excluded; those dates were not individually verified, but all fall well after 1930.

The candidate list below comes from the researchers' general knowledge. **None of these dates were verified this session**, apart from "Bye Bye Blackbird" (1926) and "By the Light of the Silvery Moon" (listed as 1909 by [pdinfo](https://www.pdinfo.com/pd-song-list/pd-song-list-best-b.php), which itself warns its lists are "NOT sufficient documentation"). Each song needs its first publication checked against sheet music before anyone records it.

## Risks, dignity and who it fits

The biggest risk is not physical harm; Cochrane recorded no serious adverse events from music activities ([CD003477](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD003477_does-music-based-therapy-help-people-dementia)). It is **making a parent feel like a child, or making them fail in front of their family.** The elderspeak research, the patronising-activity-card findings and the doll-therapy debate all point the same way, and they give Gene a usable test: *would you be comfortable if a visiting friend saw your mum using this?* Second is **overstimulation**, especially in the evening, which the calm-by-default rules address. Third is **false promise**: a family that believes the app "keeps her mind sharp" may feel let down or guilty, which is why the claims section matters as much as the code. Fourth is **substitution**: the app should be a bridge to time together, never "keeps Mum busy so you don't have to call". The Nuffield Council frames autonomy in dementia as *relational*, promoted "by encouraging relationships that are important to the person" ([Nuffield](https://www.nuffieldbioethics.org/publications/dementia/guide-to-the-report/ethical-framework)). And the person keeps the right to say no: an always-visible way out, no nagging, no notifications, and carer notes that say "If she doesn't want to, that's fine. Try another day."

**Caregiver co-use is where the evidence for benefit actually sits.** Särkämö's singing trial improved carers' quality of life as well as the person's mood ([IOS Press](https://content.iospress.com/articles/journal-of-alzheimers-disease/jad150453)). The Tailored Activity Program pilot (60 families) gave carers fewer hours "on duty" and more confidence ([Gitlin 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2682173)). The CIRCA touchscreen let people with dementia **lead the conversation and be more equal partners** than in traditional reminiscence ([CIRCA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6153376)), and a tablet built for pairs (I-CARE, only 9 pairs) reported more interaction and easier communication ([PMC9518490](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9518490)). One caution: in two reminiscence studies where carers took part jointly, carer anxiety rose at long-term follow-up ([Cochrane abstract](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD001120.pub3/pdf/CDSR/CD001120/CD001120_abstract.pdf)), so co-use is not automatically good for everyone. Practically, co-use argues for no login so a visiting son can just hand over the tablet, screens two people can read, turn-taking in Match the Socks, and a short carer page (one screen, outside the person's path) that explains the activities, the stage settings and "sing with them".

Stage fit, below, is a proposed mapping: **no source maps app types to dementia stages**, so it combines the severity–complexity link from Vahia, the guideline scoping of structured programmes to mild-to-moderate dementia ([NICE NG97](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng97/chapter/Recommendations)), Montessori and Tovertafel work in advanced dementia, and the Global Deterioration Scale's stage descriptions ([GDS](https://www.dementiaresearch.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2016/03/GDS_scale.pdf)). The carer, not the person, should choose the setting once at setup, because self-selection is unreliable.

| Stage | Garden | Match the Socks | Sing-Along | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mild (GDS 4) | Yes, though it may feel too simple | Yes, more pairs | Full lyrics, finish-the-line on, talk about the songs | Feeling patronised; offer variety, never "levels" |
| Moderate (GDS 5–6) | Yes, a strong fit | 2–4 pairs, high contrast | Large lyrics, chorus-first, listening is fine, finish-the-line optional | Getting lost; co-use matters most here |
| Severe (GDS 7) | Yes, tap-anywhere; carer may hold the device | Hide it | Listening with a carer singing; finish-the-line off | A small screen may simply not suit; an honest limit |
| Parkinson's without dementia | Yes | Yes | "Sing out!", steady beat, never scored tapping | Tremor and good-day/bad-day swings; tablet preferred |

Parkinson's needs one more flag. Evidence specific to Parkinson's disease dementia is **sparse to absent**, and even singing studies in Parkinson's find "insufficient high-quality evidence" ([Irons & Sheffield 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12645629/)), and NICE warns that in Lewy body dementia and Parkinson's disease dementia antipsychotics can worsen movement ([NICE NG97 1.7.4](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng97/chapter/Recommendations)), which makes gentle non-drug comfort matter more. Whether ambiguous shapes on screen could feed visual hallucinations in Lewy body or Parkinson's dementia was not researched; keeping images clear and avoiding shadows and creatures is a cheap precaution.

## What to test with real people first

Most of the remaining uncertainty can only be settled by watching real users, and Gene's habit of getting things onto a phone early suits that. The first round should be a handful of families, ideally including one parent with Parkinson's and one at a moderate stage of Alzheimer's, watched in person or on video, with nothing recorded by the app itself.

| Open question | Why it is open | What to watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Is 20 mm big enough on a phone for tremor? | Nunes's 14 mm floor came from smartphones; Chen suggests up to 30 mm still helps | Missed taps, double taps, frustration on a bad day vs a good day |
| What repeat-tap and wander tolerances work? | No study gives values; Apple starts at 0.1 s | Accidental double blooms, socks deselected by a second tap |
| Does tap-anywhere Garden engage anyone, and for how long? | Only uncontrolled cause-and-effect evidence | Smiles, talk, whether the carer joins in, when they stop |
| Do the socks look adult, or babyish? | No study of adult vs childlike visuals in dementia apps | Any sign of "this is for children"; the adult child's own reaction |
| Is 2–4 pairs right for moderate dementia? | Design inference | Whether the person finishes a basket, and whether they want another |
| How long should the finish-the-line pause be? | No study; 2–3 s is design judgement | Whether the person tries the line, looks worried, or ignores it |
| Do slow, low recordings feel right, or dull? | No evidence-based tempo or key | Whether people sing along, and whether they recognise the song quickly |
| Does the moving lyric highlight help or distract? | Untested | Eyes following the line vs confusion |
| Do pre-1931 songs land with people born in the 1940s–50s? | Inference from the cascading bump; untested in people now 70–95 | Recognition, singing, stories told |
| Does anyone get upset? | Music risks are qualitative only | Tears, agitation, refusal; how fast Stop is found |
| Tablet vs phone | Errors halved on tablets in one study | Whether phone use is workable at all for moderate users |

There are also loose ends in the evidence itself to tidy before any public description quotes it: confirm the Lu and Zhao horticulture paper IDs; look up the 2019 Cochrane review of cognitive training in people who already have dementia (Bahar-Fuchs et al., recalled but not verified); verify every song's first publication date; and confirm the WCAG flashing rule and Apple's 44-point minimum, which were cited from the standards rather than fetched.

## Conclusion

The evidence reshapes the Porch less than it reshapes what the Porch *says*. All three activities survive, but each for a narrower and more honest reason than its first pitch: Sing-Along because old songs outlast other memories and singing together helped both people in a real trial; Match the Socks because a familiar adult job with no way to fail answers the need for meaningful occupation, even if nobody has tested a digital one; Garden because it is the last activity that still works when the others no longer do. None of them is a treatment, and the moment the Porch implies otherwise it joins the brain-training products that regulators have already fined.

The less obvious takeaway is that the strongest benefits in this literature belong to the relationship, not the app. The trials that moved anything (singing coaching, tailored activities, CIRCA) put a family member and a person with dementia side by side, and the app was just the reason to sit there. That makes the most important design decisions the unglamorous ones: no login so a visit can start in seconds, screens big enough for two, nothing that can make a parent look like they failed, and words written as you would speak to an adult you love. If a design choice cannot pass Gene's test of setting it up on his own mother's phone, the evidence here gives no reason to keep it.
